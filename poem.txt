Kerak

My mother cooked rice in a dented pot
and let it catch on purpose.
We fought over the crust at the bottom,
gold, cracked like a rice field in August,
the part no recipe asks for.

Now the kitchen knows us.
It orders rice before the rice runs out,
measures water to the gram,
starts the cooker at six so we wake to steam,
and every grain comes out the same white,
correct, forgiven in advance.
My son has never heard a pot complain.

Tonight I tell the house to stand down.
It asks twice, the way a nurse asks.
I pour by eye. I listen
for the hiss to turn to crackle,
and wait one breath past sure.

He scrapes the bottom with a spoon,
holds up a shard the color of late light.
What's this?
The part you only get, I say,
if someone stays at the stove
long enough to ruin it a little.
